If you sell clothing on Temu and need images at exactly 1340 by 1785 pixels, the right path depends on what your source photo looks like. A clean shot on a plain background resizes in under a minute with a free online tool. A photo shot on a cluttered table, on a hanger, or on a model against a busy backdrop needs something that can isolate the product first and resize it second, which is where a dedicated checker or a desktop editor earns its keep.

If you only run a handful of listings with simple studio shots, a browser-based aspect ratio tool will do everything you need. If you're managing dozens of SKUs, working with inconsistent source photos, or getting rejections tied to background noise rather than pixel dimensions, a purpose-built image tool will save you more time than it costs. Below are the criteria that actually separate these options, not just their marketing claims.

## The criteria that actually decide this choice

Start with pixel accuracy. Temu's automated checker does not round. An export that lands at 1339x1784 or 1340x1786 can still flag as a mismatch depending on how strict the current enforcement pass is running, so you need a method that outputs the exact 1340x1785 figure rather than an approximation.

File size matters just as much. Temu's apparel image spec pairs the 1340x1785 ratio with a hard cap. Tools that list this limit explicitly, such as [the Temu image checker](/tools/temu-product-image), cite an 800px square minimum and a 3MB ceiling alongside the ratio requirement. A resize method that ignores compression will pass the dimension check and still fail on file size.

Then there's volume. Resizing one image, any tool works. Resizing 40 images a week across a growing catalog, the method that requires manual cropping on every file becomes the bottleneck, not the ratio itself.

The one most sellers miss is background and cutout handling. A plain cropping tool can change the frame dimensions, but it can't remove a shadow, fix an uneven background, or separate a garment from a hanger. That's a different job, and it changes which tool actually solves your rejection.

## Option-by-option breakdown against those criteria

Three practical paths cover most sellers: manual editing in a general image tool like Photoshop or GIMP, a free online aspect ratio calculator paired with manual export, and a purpose-built checker or desktop editor made for marketplace specs.

Manual editing in Photoshop or GIMP gives you full control over pixel dimensions, and both can output an exact 1340x1785 canvas without issue. The tradeoff is time and skill. Getting the crop right without distorting the garment, then exporting at the correct file size, takes practice. For a single hero image this is fine. For a catalog, it adds up fast, and it does nothing to fix a messy background.

A generic aspect ratio calculator solves the math problem but not the execution problem. You still need an editor to actually crop and export the file. These tools are useful for confirming what 1340x1785 reduces to as a ratio, but they don't touch file size compression or background cleanup, so they're a partial solution at best.

A dedicated marketplace image checker, like [the Temu image checker](/tools/temu-product-image), is built around the exact spec: the 1340x1785 apparel ratio, the 800px square floor, and the 3MB cap. You upload, it tells you pass or fail, and in most cases resizes to the target automatically. That removes the guesswork on pixel accuracy and file size in one step, but it still assumes your source photo is already clean. It won't cut a product out of a cluttered background for you.

A desktop AI tool, such as [AI Ecom Visual Studio](/tools/ai-ecom-visual-studio), covers the case the browser checker can't: pulling a garment off a grey or patterned background, cleaning up shadows, and then exporting to the exact ratio. This is the option for sellers whose rejections aren't really about pixels, they're about background quality that Temu's automated review also penalizes.

Sellers listing across multiple marketplaces often need more than one spec handled in the same session. If you're also prepping images for Walmart or TikTok Shop, checking against [the Walmart main image checker](/tools/walmart-main-image) or [the TikTok Shop image checker](/tools/tiktok-shop-image) in the same workflow saves you from reshooting the same product twice for different platforms.

## Verdict by scenario, and the cost of switching methods

Shooting clean, well-lit flat lays or mannequin shots on a plain background? The Temu image checker is the right call. It's fast, free to check against, and removes the pixel-math guesswork. It won't help if you're shooting on location, against fabric backdrops, or with inconsistent lighting, because the checker can't fix what the camera got wrong.

Running a small catalog and only resizing occasionally? Manual editing in Photoshop or GIMP is fine, as long as you already know the software. It stops being fine once you pass a dozen SKUs a week. Manual cropping doesn't scale, and repeated manual exports are where pixel-accuracy mistakes creep in.

If your rejections keep citing background or image quality rather than pure dimension mismatches, a desktop AI tool is the better fit, even though it means an extra download instead of a quick browser check. For a seller with one or two clean product shots and no background issues, that extra tool is overkill, and a browser checker does the job faster.

Switching between these methods costs little. None of them lock your source files into a proprietary format, and moving from manual editing to a checker, or from a checker to a desktop tool, just means re-uploading the same original photo. The real cost is the time spent learning a new interface, which for most of these tools is under ten minutes.

A quick worked example makes this concrete. Say your source photo is 2000x2667 pixels, already close to the 1340x1785 ratio of 0.75. Dividing 1340 by 1785 gives 0.7507, and dividing 2000 by 2667 gives 0.7499, close enough that a straightforward resize works without aggressive cropping. If your source photo is instead 2000x2000, a perfect square, resizing to 1340x1785 without cropping stretches the garment and distorts it, which is a common cause of rejected or visually wrong listings even when the pixel dimensions technically pass.

## FAQ

### Why does Temu require 1340x1785 for clothing images?

The 1340x1785 figure simplifies to a tall portrait ratio that fits Temu's apparel listing template, keeping garment photos consistent across the catalog grid. Temu's seller documentation outlines image specifications by category, and clothing listings are held to this taller portrait format rather than the square format used for general product shots.

### Can I use a different aspect ratio if I crop the image?

No. Temu's automated check looks for the specific ratio, not just "close enough." A photo cropped to a similar but not identical ratio, like 4:5 exactly instead of 1340:1785, can still fail automated review. Always export to the exact pixel dimensions rather than approximating the ratio by eye.

### What is the best file format for Temu product images?

JPEG is the standard choice for marketplace product photos because it balances file size and image quality well under common caps. Keep the file under the 3MB limit noted for Temu apparel images, since even a correctly sized image can fail if the file itself is too large.

### How do I fix a 'low quality' rejection on Temu?

Low quality flags usually point to compression artifacts, poor lighting, or a messy background rather than the pixel dimensions themselves. Re-export at a higher quality setting before resizing, and if the background is the real problem, a tool built for cutouts will fix more than repeated manual cropping will.
