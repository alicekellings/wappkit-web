# Pre-Launch Checklist for Small Software Sites: What to Include Before Going Live

A small software site needs a working download link, a clear product description, a buy or activation path, basic legal pages, and a support contact before it goes live. Skip the 150-point enterprise checklists. For a desktop utility or indie web tool, a lean pre-launch checklist focuses on the few elements that directly affect trust, downloads, and license delivery. If a visitor cannot figure out what the tool does, download it, or pay for it, nothing else on your site matters.

This workflow is built for solo founders and small teams preparing to launch a desktop utility, a web app, or a small SaaS product. It targets the practical launch readiness milestones that actually move the needle: clear messaging, reliable installers, working license key activation, and basic analytics. It is not built for complex enterprise platforms with multi-tier approval flows or regulated compliance needs. Use this guide to close the gap between a half-finished marketing page and a product that is ready to accept paying customers.

## What You Need Before Starting

Before running through the checklist, you need a product build that is stable enough for external users. This means your compiled binary, executable, or web deployment is functionally complete. You are not adding new features while checking these boxes. You also need a registered domain with DNS properly configured, an SSL certificate installed, and a hosting environment capable of serving static files or running your application stack.

You also need to decide on your payment and licensing providers before touching the website. If you are selling a desktop tool, you need a payment processor like Stripe or Paddle, and a method for generating and delivering license keys. If you are building a web utility, you need an authentication mechanism. Do not leave these decisions for the final hour. Integrating license key activation into your site requires coordination between your payment gateway, your licensing system, and your application code. Having these accounts and API keys ready prevents the most common launch day delays.

## The Simplest Workflow That Still Works

The core pre-launch workflow for a small software site breaks down into a single sequence. Each step exists to remove a specific point of friction between a visitor arriving and becoming a satisfied, licensed user.

1. **Finalize the one-sentence value proposition.** Place it at the top of your homepage. Visitors should know exactly what your desktop tool does within three seconds. If your headline is clever but vague, you will lose users before they ever reach the download button.
2. **Verify the download flow.** Upload your final build to your [download center](/download). Click the link on a clean machine, ideally a fresh operating system install or a virtual machine with no development tools installed. Run the installer and confirm it does not trigger unexpected warnings or require missing dependencies.
3. **Test the purchase and license retrieval path.** Buy your own product using a test credit card. Confirm the email receipt arrives. Click the license retrieval link and confirm it generates a valid key. Paste that key into your application and verify the license activation unlocks the paid features. If this path breaks, you lose money.
4. **Add the required legal pages.** You need a Privacy Policy explaining what data you collect, a Terms of Service outlining usage rights, and a Refund Policy. For desktop utilities, explicitly state what telemetry the app sends home. Users are increasingly wary of background processes.
5. **Install basic analytics.** Set up a privacy-friendly analytics tool like Plausible or Fathom. You need to know where traffic comes from and which pages cause drop-offs. Avoid heavy tracking scripts that slow down your site.
6. **Configure error logging and uptime monitoring.** Connect your site to an uptime monitor like UptimeRobot. Set up an error tracker like Sentry if your application runs complex JavaScript or a backend server. You need to know immediately if your site goes down or throws errors.
7. **Test the mobile experience.** Even if you sell a desktop utility, buyers will research it on their phones. Ensure your homepage, pricing page, and [license center](/license) render correctly on small screens and that buttons are easy to tap.

This sequence works because it follows the user journey from awareness to activation. You start with messaging, move to the product delivery, verify the monetization loop, establish legal trust, and finally set up the instruments to measure post-launch performance. Skipping any step creates a leak. Skipping the clean machine test means you might ship a broken installer. Skipping the license test means you might ship a broken payment loop.

## Where the Workflow Breaks or Gets Noisy

Indie founders frequently overbuild specific areas while ignoring critical infrastructure. The most common failure point is spending weeks perfecting visual design elements while leaving the download or licensing flow untested. A beautiful site with a broken license key activation system generates zero revenue. Prioritize functional checkout and delivery over pixel perfection.

The workflow gets noisy when founders attempt to build custom licensing systems from scratch. Writing your own license key generator, validation server, and webhook handler is a massive distraction. It introduces security risks, maintenance burdens, and edge cases you will not anticipate. This is where using a dedicated platform makes sense. A system like [Wappkit](/) handles the heavy lifting of license delivery and activation, letting you focus on the product itself.

Another failure point is ignoring edge cases in the install process. Your installer might work perfectly on your developer machine but fail on a user's locked-down corporate laptop. Test your installer on different operating system versions. Check what happens when a user lacks administrator privileges. Document these requirements clearly on your download page so users know what to expect before they run the file.

## How to Review the Output or Results

Once you complete the workflow, you need to verify the results. Do not assume the checklist passing means the site is flawless. You must actively review the output of each step.

First, check your analytics dashboard after a small batch of test traffic. Send the link to five trusted peers. Look at where they drop off. If everyone exits on the pricing page, your pricing might be unclear or your value proposition weak. If they drop off on the download page, your file host might be slow or the instructions confusing.

Second, review your error logs. Even a simple static site can throw 404 errors for missing favicon files or broken asset links. A dynamic application might surface database connection errors under load. Clear these errors before a public launch. Search engines and users notice broken pages.

Third, audit your email delivery. Check your spam folder after sending test purchase receipts and license retrieval emails. If your transactional emails land in spam, paying customers will complain that they never received their keys. Configure SPF, DKIM, and DMARC records for your domain to ensure reliable email delivery.

## When to Use a Dedicated Tool Instead of Doing It Manually

Building a software site manually using static site generators or basic HTML gives you maximum control. However, it requires you to manually handle updates, security patches, and infrastructure. As your desktop tools portfolio grows, manual management becomes a liability.

You should use a dedicated tool or platform when you need to manage multiple products, complex licensing rules, or versioned downloads. If you plan to offer tiered pricing, upgrade paths, or volume licensing, a custom solution becomes a burden. Platforms designed for software distribution handle these edge cases natively. They manage the database of licensed users, handle webhook failures from payment processors, and provide a secure [license center](/license) for users to retrieve lost keys.

Using a dedicated platform also helps with discoverability and trust. A site hosted on a recognized platform benefits from shared infrastructure and established trust signals. If you are spending more time maintaining your website than improving your software, you need to switch to a managed solution. The goal is to get back to building the actual product.

## FAQ

### What is the minimum viable content for a small software site?
A homepage with a clear value proposition, a download link, a pricing page, a contact or support method, and basic legal pages. Everything else is optional for launch day.

### Do I need a blog before launching my software product?
No. A blog helps with long-term SEO and organic traffic, but it is not a launch requirement. Launch with a functional product site and add content marketing later once you have user feedback.

### How do I handle license key delivery before launch?
Use a payment processor that supports webhook delivery, or use a dedicated software platform that handles license generation automatically. Send the key via email and provide a web page where users can retrieve lost keys.

### What is the most common pre-launch mistake for indie software?
Focusing on visual polish while neglecting the download and license activation flow. A broken payment or delivery path stops revenue immediately.

### Should I build my own download and licensing system or use a platform?
Use a platform unless your software has highly specific licensing requirements. Building a custom system takes time away from product development and introduces security and maintenance risks.
